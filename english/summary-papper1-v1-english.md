# Summary of [1]

- Paper: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11741v3
- Experiment code: https://github.com/KempnerInstitute/chess-research
- Official online summary of the work: https://transcendence.eddie.win/ 

## Main idea
- A model is trained on chess games to predict the next move.
- Filtering out players with rating $\le 1000$, it plays at a rating of $\approx 1500$.
- The author calls this phenomenon ("playing better than the best expert you trained on") *transcendence*.
- It only happens when sampling at low temperature.
- The **hypothesis** is that low-rated players make *blunders* that are not shared (uncorrelated errors; the paper calls them *idiosyncratic*). That is why it calls them noisy experts: deep down they are experts, but with noise. Low-temperature sampling is equivalent to a majority vote, which has a *denoising* effect.
- It does not happen when filtering at rating $\le 1500$: a 1000-rated player can be thought of as a noisy 1500-rated player, but a 1500-rated player cannot be thought of as a noisy 2000-rated player.
- Another hypothesis, empirically supported, is that *dataset diversity* is necessary.

## Formalization and theoretical predictions
- $\mathcal{X}$: input space: game prefixes.
- $\mathcal{Y}$: finite output space (moves). For Connect4 $|\mathcal{Y}|=7$, including illegal moves (full columns). Valid policies assign them probability 0.
- $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$ the set of all probability distributions over $\mathcal{Y}$.
- $\mathcal{F}$: functions $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$, that is, a function that, given a game prefix, computes a probability distribution for the next move: $f(y \mid x)$ (also called a policy).
- We denote by $k$ the number of experts. Each expert defines a policy; the $k$ **experts** are $f_1, \dots, f_k \in \mathcal{F}$.
- Input distribution $p$ over $\mathcal{X}$ seen during training: how likely it is to observe the game prefix $x$. Note: $p$ depends on the policies that were used to generate the games.
- Analogously, $p_{\text{test}}$ will be the data distribution used when evaluating the model.
- We will call the **mixture of experts**, denoted $f$:
$$
f(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} f_i(y \mid x) \tag{1}
$$
- During training, the learner will learn from the joint distribution $D(x,y) = p(x)\, f(y \mid x)$. This is equivalent to saying that the formulation assumes the data are generated by sampling $x \sim p$ and then choosing an expert **uniformly at random** to label it.

The formalization is an idealization.
In practice [1] uses real data where
- the expert is not drawn per position but is the same player throughout the whole game
- the players are not equally represented
- positions are not drawn but reached by playing.

- **Definition: Reward** $r: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}$, is an external function that says how good a move is (only for evaluating, not for training; see the section *improvement analysis*).

- We define the "expected reward" metric of a policy $f$ on $p_{\text{test}}$:
$$
R_{p_{\text{test}}}(f) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{test}}}\mathbb{E}_{y \sim f(\cdot \mid x)}\big[ r(x,y) \big] 
$$

It is the metric used in the theoretical formulation of transcendence.
However, the main result of the paper does not use it as a quality measure: it uses the rating from 300 games against Stockfish.

The proofs in [1] assume that $\forall x$, $r(x,\cdot)$ is not constant in $y$. It is a stronger assumption than they need: it would suffice that No big effect on the proofs; actually it would suffice that $\exists x$ where it is not constant with $p(x)>0$.

In our work we call those states non-trivial (in Connect4 roughly half of the states are trivial). And we evaluate reward only on them.

The "favor" analysis does use it. In practice it is computed by making all valid moves and evaluating Stockfish's heuristic on each new state to compute the probability of losing/drawing/winning.


- **The learner**, trained ideally (infinite data, infinite capacity), is defined as:
$$
\hat f = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\, H(f, \cdot)
$$
Where $H$ is the cross-entropy loss function.

- **Theorem:** the minimizer is exactly the mixture: $\hat f = f$.

- **Definition: Transcendence** A configuration $(f_1, \dots, f_k, p)$ exhibits transcendence if the trained learner outperforms the **best individual expert**:

$$
R_{p_{\text{test}}}(\hat f) > \max_{i \in [k]} R_{p_{\text{test}}}(f_i) \tag{3}
$$

- **Theorem:** At temperature 1 transcendence is impossible. Since the model learns the mixture and the reward is linear in the distribution, an average never exceeds the maximum.
- **Theorem:** low temperature is a necessary and sufficient condition for transcendence.
At $\tau = 0$, the arg-max of the mixture $f$ is taken, which is equivalent to a majority vote.

- **Theorem:** in a setting where the data are generated by a noisy expert (plays randomly with probability $\rho$ and perfectly with $1-\rho$), there is transcendence at a sufficiently low temperature.
- **Theorem:**  in a setting where the state space is partitioned into $k$ regions and the data are generated by $k$ complementary experts (expert $i$ plays perfectly in its region $X_i$ and randomly outside it), there is transcendence at a sufficiently low temperature, as long as the test distribution touches at least two regions.

Note: these theorems are based on the theoretical setup. In it, the state distribution $p(x)$ is something external, fixed, and independent of the experts' policies. In reality, and especially with experts that have different policies as in this last theorem, it makes sense that the states visited by each expert are different, because the expert's own policy will lead the game to different positions. [1] acknowledges this as a simplification, and [2] does take it into account in its formalization with a $p_i$ per expert.

- **Definition: Improvement (or *favor*)** of a policy $f'$ with respect to $f$ at position $x$:

$$F(f', f; x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim f'(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)] - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim f(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)] = r_x(f') - r_x(f)$$

It expresses how much better the expected reward of what $f'$ plays is than that of what $f$ would have played in that same position.

It is used in the *improvement analysis* experiment (below), which tries to shed light on how the improvement in reward is distributed. Intuitively: does the gain come from improving a little in many states, or improving a lot in few states? The hypothesis is that low-rated players play well almost all the time and ruin the game with isolated blunders.

- **Definition: Global improvement (or global *favor*)**:  is the average of this improvement over the states visited when playing with $f'$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim d_{f'}}\big[F(f', f; x)\big]$$
where $d_{f'}$ is the state visitation distribution when following $f'$. The choice of $d_{f'}$ rather than $d_f$ is inspired by the *Performance Difference Lemma* from *Reinforcement Learning*.

## Experiments: details, results and conclusions

### Building the training set

- The theoretical formulation assumes $p(x) \geq 0 \ \forall x$ (otherwise the learner could not learn anything about it).
- The theoretical formulation treats $p$ as fixed and external:
>"We leave a complete analysis of a more general setting to future work"). 
- In the experiments there are three datasets $D_1 \subseteq D_2 \subseteq D3$ where games are filtered by considering only those in which the highest-rated participant has rating 1000, 1300, and 1500 respectively.
- This development set is split into training/validation 95/5 at the game level. The validation set is used for monitoring; there is no early stopping.

### Definition of the evaluation method

- Glicko-2 rating from playing 300 games against Stockfish levels 1, 3 and 5, half with white and half with black.
- They report $R \pm 2\,RD$ (which corresponds to a 95% confidence interval)
- Temperatures evaluated: 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5.
- Long games (more than 89 moves) are declared a draw
- The limitations section points out:
> "our theoretical framework assumes that game conditions at test time match those seen during training"

#### How the rating is computed:
- The Elo rating is computed by having the trained model play against Stockfish levels 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, whose ratings are known (1554, 1759, 1867, 2006, and 2148).
- The Stockfish ratings and the win rate against them are used to compute the rating of the trained model.
- The Stockfish ratings are not something readily available: the paper itself pre-computes them by having those Stockfish levels play against Maia bots (another heuristic, which does imitate human players of a given Elo).


#### On game sampling

In the formulation the model predicts the next move.
But in practice the model predicts a character.
A move is several characters.
Sampling a move works like this:
- 10 characters are generated.
- One character is sampled at a time, according to the distribution computed by the trained model (no sophisticated decoding such as beam search is used).
- The next word is taken (the separator between moves is the space in PGN notation).
- It is repeated if the model generates an illegal move (5 attempts, otherwise it loses).
This subtlety is important because it creates a gap between the theoretical formulation and practice: the per-character arg-max is not necessarily the per-move arg-max

### Training regime
- **Model:** Karpathy's nanoGPT with 50M parameters.
- **Training objective**: next-token prediction
- **Input representation and tokenization**: PGN strings (`1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 ...`), character-level tokenization (32 symbols).
It never sees the board or the rules, only the text of the moves and the result (note: this is confusing; the paper says it sees the result, but the code they share does not include it). It receives neither rating nor reward during training.
- **Data:** ~1B human games from lichess.org (Jan–Oct 2023). Three datasets are created by filtering by $\text{max}(\text{rating}_1,\text{rating}_2) \leq L_{\text{dataset}}$ with $L_{\text{dataset}} \in \{1000, 1300, 1500\}$

- **Training regime and compute used**
    - Optimizer: AdamW, lr 3e-4 with cosine decay down to 3e-5, 2000 warmup steps, weight decay 0.1, β=(0.9, 0.95), grad clip 1.0, bf16.
    - In practice, training was stopped before the learning rate schedule finished, so it is as if it had been trained with a learning rate of ~3e-4
    - Activation: the paper says ReLU, the code uses GELU (nanoGPT). No bias, dropout 0, 16 layers, 8 heads, 512 dims, vocab 32, block 1023.
    - Gradient descent steps: 100,000
    - Mini-batch size: the paper states 125K tokens, actually ~128K = 1023 block_size (tokens/sequence) . 125 batch_size (sequences/batch).
    - Tokens seen: 100K × 128K ≈ 13B tokens
    - gradient_accumulation_steps, confusing evidence: the paper states 1 and the code shows 4
    - The model never gets to see all of the 1B-game dataset.
    - Compute: between 6 and 12 h per model using 1 H100 80GB. But the code shows that they trained with DDP on 4 GPUs.
The 3 trained models (on the 3 filtered datasets) are on Hugging Face.
The repository has the configs of the transformer architecture specifically.

### Explanatory analyses

#### Improvement analysis (favor)

- They play 100 games with the already-trained model at low temperature (0.001) against Stockfish to fix the evaluation dataset. Only the positions where it was the model's turn to play are taken.
- On that set, they sample what it would have played at temperature 0.001, 0.75 and 1 (100 samples per move are taken).
- With approximately 38.2 moves per game, this gives 382,000 counterfactual moves: $38.2 \times 100 \times 100$.
- The improvement is computed using the formulation above, calculating $r(x,y)$ with Stockfish's heuristic, which returns the probability of winning in the state $x'$ that results from playing $y$ in $x$.
- **Result:** they plot the histogram of the distribution of the "improvement": the x axis is the favor and the y axis is the relative frequency of the states showing that "improvement". Visually it confirms that it is right-skewed with a long tail, supporting the hypothesis: the gain comes from a few key states.

![Histogram of the favor F at temperature 0.001 and 0.75 relative to temperature 1](../referencias/mi-review-de-literatura/img/zhang2024/adv-gain-dist-flat.png)


- Illegal samples are counted with reward 0. At temperature 1 there are positions with up to 35 out of 100 samples illegal, and at 0.001 almost none. That inflates the favor in favor of low temperature, because in real play an illegal move could be resampled indefinitely (not just 5 times).

Conceptual note: it references the *Performance Difference Lemma* from *Reinforcement Learning*, which says that the performance difference between two policies is the advantage averaged over the states visited by the new policy. That is why the 100 games are built by having the model play at low temperature.

#### Analysis of generalization to unseen states

- The theory assumes the experts are defined over all of $\mathcal{X}$. 
- In chess, after about move 16 almost every board appears only once in the dataset ([1] confirms this empirically in its repository)
- Nevertheless, transcendence appears. Why?
- **Hypothesis of [1]:** the model compresses games into a latent representation shared between *similar* positions. Denoising also acts at this level, so it extends to states that are unseen but *similar* to seen ones.
- **Experiment:** they project the last hidden layer vector to 2D (using t-SNE). They plot each point with a color that depends on the probability that White wins according to Stockfish. Observation: the probability that the current player wins (what we defined as reward) is not used, because that quantity flips at every change of turn, and the goal is for the color of a game to evolve continuously as the moves go by.
- **Result:** clusters form where decided positions (reward 0, 1) and balanced positions (reward 0.5) end up together, respectively.
- **Conclusion:** the latent representation captures the relative advantage of the positions and the identity of the players.

![t-SNE of the last hidden layer colored by reward, with four example boards](../referencias/mi-review-de-literatura/img/zhang2024/latent_board_state_reward_tsne.png)


#### Analysis of dataset diversity and entropy

- In the dataset with Elo up to 1500 transcendence is not observed. Why?
- **Hypothesis:** A 1000-rated player can be thought of as a noisy 1500, but a 1500 cannot be thought of as a noisy 2000.
- The proposal is to measure the diversity of each dataset as the **normalized entropy of the action distribution**

$$
H_f(Y \mid X) = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{y \sim f(y \mid x = X)}\big[-\log_2 f(y \mid x = X)\big]}{\log_2 |\mathcal{Y}|} \in [0, 1]
$$
- **Result:** $\text{entropy}(D_{1000}) > \text{entropy}(D_{1300}) > \text{entropy}(D_{1500})$. The dataset that did not transcend is the least diverse.

![Histogram of the normalized entropy of the action distribution over common states, for the three datasets](../referencias/mi-review-de-literatura/img/zhang2024/entropy_of_action_distribution_over_common_states.png)

- **Conclusion:** diversity is a necessary condition for transcendence.
- Implementation details:
    - They define a common position as one that appears more than 100 times. (position = board, not PGN prefix)
    - For each one they compute the entropy of its move distribution (see formula below).
    - They average those entropies (unweighted).
    - In the paper they say they normalize by the legal moves, but in the code they do it by the number of distinct moves observed in that position within the dataset:
$$
H(x) = \frac{-\sum_{y \in A(x)} \hat f(y \mid x)\, \log_2 \hat f(y \mid x)}{\log_2 |A(x)|}, \qquad A(x) = \{\, y : \hat f(y \mid x) > 0 \,\}
$$

This could inflate the entropy of positions with few appearances

- Subtleties and criticisms of the implementation in [1]:
    - In the paper they state the limitation that they only cover openings and endgames (since those are the only ones that can be common). But in their actual implementation they cut off after move 16
    - It compares a metric across datasets, but the common positions depend on the dataset since the datasets are nested (it uses the same constant 100 for all 3 even though each one includes the previous). In $D_{1500}$ the common positions have more appearances, therefore more distinct moves observed, therefore a larger denominator and lower normalized entropy, even if the underlying distribution were identical.
    - The average is unweighted; it is not the conditional entropy from the theory.
    - There is a simpler explanation of the result that does not involve diversity:  $D_{1500}$ has fewer errors to cancel and the ceiling for transcendence is closer.
    

## Evidence outside chess

- **SQuAD v2 (natural language QA):** pretrained LLMs answer reading comprehension questions better at low temperature. 
It shows that lowering the temperature helps, but it does not measure transcendence: there are no experts to compare against.


- **Linear toy model:** 
    - It builds a synthetic dataset.
    - x are 100-dimensional Gaussian vectors.
    - the true label is determined as $y = \arg\max_i W^{*}_i x$, with $W^{*}$ a fixed $10 \times d$ matrix (one linear separator per class)
    - $k = 5$ experts are noisy copies of that separator: expert $j$ labels with $W_j = W^{*} + \xi_j$, where each entry of $\xi_j$ is independent $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$.
    - 10K examples labeled by random experts
    - A linear model is trained with cross-entropy loss.
    - The model's test accuracy is measured at different temperatures and compared with that of the best expert. 
    - **Result:** there is transcendence at low temperature when $\sigma$ is large, and none when $\sigma$ is small.
    - It replicates the theoretical formulation better: unlike what happens in the games, $p$ is Gaussian, fixed and external. And the experts are uniformly represented in the dataset.
    - **Gaps in this toy setting:**
        - The experts' errors are never correlated: the toy model only covers the independent-error case.
        -  $\sigma$ affects two things at once: how much each expert errs and how much the experts differ from each other. The result "with small $\sigma$ there is no transcendence" does not prove whether it is due to lack of diversity or because the best expert is already nearly perfect.


### Positioning of my work with respect to [1]

#### Decisions

- Regarding $p$ vs $p_{\text{test}}$, what matters to us is that we evaluate *transcendence* properly: comparing the model against the experts.
- It is not correct to compare reward across configurations (because they have different distributions). But it is correct to compare *transcendence* across configurations.
- The "score against the bot" metric is correct for comparing configurations.

#### Limitations of [1] that I will try to cover
- Entropy is only measured on common states (openings/endgames), not in the middlegame where games are decided.
- They do not distinguish transcendence on seen vs unseen states.
- [1] assumes the same $p$ for all experts, full support ($p(x)>0 \ \forall x$) and experts sampled uniformly at each state. But each expert's policy influences which states it visits, inducing a different $p_{\text{expert}}$. The model could learn a mixture weighted by $P(\text{expert} \mid x)$ depending on the state, so transcendence could also arise from Bayesian weighting (not only from the majority vote, which is what the paper studies).
- [1] is aware of this limitation and points it out. And it clarifies that the work does not cover composition or reasoning.
- [1] clarifies that there is no evidence that the model produces solutions a human could not devise. Our work will not study this either, since our learning is also by imitation; we do not expect discovery.
- The analyses in [1] support its hypothesis that blunders (errors) occur in few positions and are decorrelated across experts (which is why denoising works). But they do not study whether those blunders are systematic errors of the same player or casual slips; all they care about is that they are uncorrelated across different experts. We can study both scenarios.

#### Methodological checks

- Add a fixed reference test set across configurations if we want to compare metrics across configurations.
- Report the seen/unseen fraction for train vs test for each configuration and against the reference test set.
- Quantify in some way how much $p$ moves between configurations.

- I would **NOT** create a $p$ with a fixed generator.

### DOUBTS

- The model cannot identify which expert is playing from the prefix (the policy conditions the whole game). Could this be a problem for the model learning "the average of the experts"?
- For experiment 2 (playing by regions), sample per move. If you choose per game, the experts may end up with different $p$ among themselves.
