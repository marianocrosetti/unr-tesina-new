# Experiments 2, 3 and 4

This document defines experiments 2 (skill selection), 3 (seen vs. unseen positions) and 4 (generalization with disjoint support). They cover specific objectives 2 to 5 of proposal v2.2. Objective 3 (temperature dependence) is not a separate experiment: it is answered with the τ sweep of experiments 1 and 2, and it is made explicit in the experiment 2 section.

Everything defined in the section "Elements common to all experiments" of `experimento1/experimento1-v1.md` applies unchanged: domain, solver, notation (`state`, `move`, `reward`, `non-trivial states`), dataset and vocabulary, model and hyperparameters, stopping rule, metrics (`acc_τ`, `E[r]_τ`, `score`) and temperature sweep. Here we only define what changes with respect to that: the expert population, the variables and constants of each configuration, the additional evaluations and the predictions.

A convention repeated in all three: the pre-proposal exploration already ran a version of each one (findings 2, 3 and 6 of `trascendencia_slides.html`). Where the design here differs from that of the exploration, it is indicated with **Note**. Predictions are recorded before running, as in experiment 1.

# Experiment 2: skill selection

## Objective

Quantify transcendence through the *selection* mechanism \[2\] by varying the proportion of data generated by the competent expert, and contrast its dependence on temperature with that of the error-cancellation mechanism of experiment 1 (objective 3 of the proposal).

## Proposed design

### Conceptual description

In experiment 1 all experts are equally good everywhere and their errors are either shared or independent. Here, instead, each expert is competent in one region of the state space and outside it makes the same error as the other non-competent experts. Since the error outside the region is fully shared, the vote of experiment 1 cannot cancel it: the only way for the model to play well in a state is for the expert competent in that state to have generated the majority of the data there. That is what the variable `α` controls: how much more each expert speaks about what it knows. The idea is to vary `α` and see from which point the model surpasses the best expert.

### More formal definition

* `K` experts and a partition of the set of states into `K` regions `R_1, …, R_K`. It is a constant fixed at `K = 4`.
* Expert `i` in a non-trivial state `e`:
  * If `e ∈ R_i`: plays an optimal move at random.
  * If `e ∉ R_i`: plays `w(e)`, the same shared erroneous move defined in experiment 1 (the leftmost legal column that is not optimal).
  * In trivial states it plays a legal move at random (as in experiment 1).
* **Routing** `α ∈ [0, 1]`: in a state `e ∈ R_j`, the move is generated by the competent expert `j` with probability `α + (1 − α)/K`, and by each of the other `K − 1` experts with probability `(1 − α)/K`. `α = 0` is uniform routing (any expert generates the move with the same probability, regardless of the state); `α = 1` is perfect routing (the competent one always generates it).
  It is the variable to sweep between configurations: `α ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.45, 0.7, 1}`.
* **Construction of the regions**: the region of a state is `hash(state) mod K`, with a deterministic hash of the board.
  **Note:** unlike experiment 1, here it does not matter whether the region is representable. The mixture in *every* non-trivial state is the same: mass `α + (1 − α)/K` on the optimal move and mass `(1 − α)(K − 1)/K` on `w(e)`. The model does not need to know which region it is in to reproduce the argmax of the mixture; it only needs, as in experiment 1, to be able to represent `w(e)`. That is why the exploration's hash is kept for the regions and only `w(e)` is changed to match experiment 1 (the exploration used an erroneous move defined by a hash).
* Both players of each game are generated with the same population and the same routing, as in experiment 1.

### Theoretical prediction

* Each individual expert is correct only in its region: `acc(expert) ≈ 1/K = 0.25` over non-trivial states. The "best expert" is all `K` at once, by symmetry.
* At `τ = 1` the model imitates the mixture: `acc_1 ≈ α + (1 − α)/K`, which exceeds `1/K` for every `α > 0`. That is: **there is transcendence at temperature 1 for every `α > 0`**. This is what \[2\] claims for the selection mechanism and what Proposition 1 of \[1\] forbids for error cancellation. It is the central contrast for objective 3.
* At `τ → 0` the model plays the argmax of the mixture, which is optimal if and only if `α + (1 − α)/K > (1 − α)(K − 1)/K`, that is:

  `α > α* = (K − 2)/(2K − 2)`; for `K = 4`, `α* = 1/3`.

  Above the threshold, lowering the temperature takes `acc` to 1 and amplifies the gain. **Below the threshold, lowering the temperature worsens the model** (it commits it to `w(e)`), and at `τ → 0` it ends up *below* the best expert. This is the falsifiable prediction that \[2\] does not formulate and that is derived from Proposition 2 of \[1\].
* Prediction on the finite model (recorded before running): the exploration saw the sign change between `α = 0.2` and `α = 0.45`, and a smooth transition around `α*` instead of a step. That is why the sweep includes `0.3`, `0.4` and `0.45`: the shape of the transition is what a finite model adds to the theory.

### Configurations

| Constants | Variables |
|---|---|
| `K = 4`, `w(e)` as in exp. 1, regions by hash, data size and training regime as in exp. 1 | `α ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.45, 0.7, 1}` (7 configurations) |

### Evaluation (what is added to the common metrics)

* The three common metrics are reported for the whole `τ` sweep. The main figure is `acc_τ` and `E[r]_τ` as a function of `α` for `τ = 1` and `τ → 0`, with the best-expert line and the vertical line at `α*`.
* **Temperature curve by mechanism** (objective 3): in a single figure, gain over the best expert as a function of `τ` for one configuration of experiment 1 (`P = 0`) and two of this experiment (one with `α < α*` and another with `α > α*`). The prediction is that the three curves have qualitatively different shapes: in cancellation the gain appears only when lowering `τ`; in selection above the threshold it is already positive at `τ = 1` and grows as `τ` is lowered; in selection below the threshold it is positive at `τ = 1` and decreases until it becomes negative.
* For the `score` against the bot, the bot plays the policy of the *routed* population (the mixture), which is what generated the data.

### Optional control: complementary experts (Theorem 4 of \[1\])

Same design but, outside its region, each expert plays a random legal move instead of `w(e)` (independent error instead of shared), with `α = 0`. This is the case that \[1\] proves transcends at `τ → 0`. It serves to show that the source of the difficulty in experiment 2 is that the error outside the region is shared. One configuration. # Experiment 3: seen vs. unseen positions

## Objective

Quantify what part of the transcendence occurs in positions that appear in training and what part in new positions, and how that decomposition changes with the amount of data (objective 4 of the proposal). Neither \[1\] nor \[2\] measures this: \[2\] evaluates cancellation and selection on seen queries and generalization on unseen ones with error-free experts.

## Proposed design

### Conceptual description

Proposition 2 of \[1\] is a statement *per state*: the argmax of the mixture in state `e`. A finite model can only estimate that mixture in `e` if it saw `e` (several times) during training. In new positions there are no votes: what the model does is generalize what it learned in similar positions. This experiment trains nothing new: it takes the models of experiment 1, labels each test state as seen or unseen, and reports the metrics separately. The only variable is the number of training games, to see how the gain on unseen states depends on the data.

### More formal definition

* **Seen state**: a test state `e` is *seen* if the same board appears in the training set (not necessarily through the same sequence of moves: the domain admits transpositions). The comparison is by board, not by sequence prefix.
* **Seen fraction** `f_v`: fraction of the non-trivial test states that are seen. It is reported per configuration; it is the operational measure of the overlap between training and test.
* **Gain decomposition**: for each metric `m ∈ {acc_τ, E[r]_τ}` and its gain over the expert `g = m(model) − m(expert)`, it holds that

  `g = f_v · g_seen + (1 − f_v) · g_unseen`

  with each term computed over the corresponding subset. All three are reported.
* **Theoretical ceiling**: `m(argmax of the true mixture)`, computed analytically in each test state with the distribution of the experts (which is known by construction). It is what a model with infinite data would reach at `τ → 0`. It is reported on seen and unseen states separately.
* **Phase**: opening if `t < 8`, middlegame if `8 ≤ t < 20`, endgame if `t ≥ 20` (same cutoffs as the exploration). TODO: confirm the cutoffs with the real game-length distribution.
* Variable: number of training games `N ∈ {20K, 80K, 320K}`, keeping the number of epochs fixed (the steps scale with `N`) and the stopping rule of experiment 1.
  **Note:** experiment 1 leaves "epochs to be determined" and contemplates generating more data. If experiment 1 ends up using `N` greater than 80K, this sweep is run around that value (`N/4`, `N`, `4N`).

### Configurations

| Constants | Variables |
|---|---|
| Population of experiment 1 with `P = 0` (all errors independent: it is where the vote has the most to gain), `ρ = 0.3`, model and regime of exp. 1 | `N ∈ {20K, 80K, 320K}` (3 configurations; the 80K one is that of exp. 1 with `P = 0`, it is not retrained) |

The same decomposition is also applied, with no new runs, to the `P = 1` configurations of experiment 1 (rule) and to its hash contrast cell. This is what separates correlation from representability: in biased states `B` with the rule, `acc → 0` on seen and on unseen states; with the hash, `acc → 0` on seen but `acc > 0` on unseen, because the model cannot know that a new state is in `B` and plays what it would play in a similar state.

### Evaluation (what is added to the common metrics)

* Main table: for each `N`, total gain, gain on seen, gain on unseen, `f_v` and theoretical ceiling, at `τ → 0`.
* All of the above stratified by phase. Caution already noted in the proposal: the seen fraction falls with depth (openings repeat, endgames almost never), so seen/unseen is correlated with phase. Without the stratification, what is an effect of depth would be attributed to memorization.
* **Third population of states, optional**: the states that the model itself reaches when playing against the bot (the 300 games of the `score`). Over them, `E[r]` is computed for the model and for the counterfactual expert in the same states. The exploration found that `score` and per-state `E[r]` can disagree (a configuration beat the bot and at the same time had a lower per-state `E[r]` than the expert on its own trajectories): the outcome of a game depends on *when* the errors are made, not only on how many. It is the reason the three common metrics are always reported. TODO: decide whether it goes into the thesis or remains as an observation.

### Predictions (recorded before running)

* On seen states, the gain reaches the theoretical ceiling already with `N = 20K`.
* On unseen states, the gain is negative with little data (the model is worse than the expert in new positions), approaches zero with `N = 80K` and becomes positive with `N = 320K`.
* The total gain is the combination weighted by `f_v`, and `f_v` grows slowly with `N` (the exploration saw 0.41, 0.47 and 0.53).
* This explains the "quarter of the ceiling" of experiment 1: the ceiling is reached where there are votes, and generalization only contributes with abundant data. It is also a reading of the result of \[1\]: with one pass over 10⁹ games, the new-positions component is the one they did have.

# Experiment 4: generalization with disjoint support (composition)

## Objective

Quantify transcendence through the *generalization* mechanism \[2\], where \[2\] obtained weak results, with two subpopulations of experts whose supports do not overlap: one that only shows openings and one that only shows endgames after openings of a different style (objective 5 of the proposal). It is the gap that \[1\] explicitly names: its theory assumes that each expert is defined over the whole state space, which is "impossible after move 15".

## Proposed design

### Conceptual description

Family A plays perfect openings and its games are cut at move `N`: it never shows an endgame. Family B opens with a restricted style (only edge columns) and plays the perfect endgame. No training sequence shows an endgame after a good opening. The question is whether the model, itself playing a good opening (learned from A), knows how to play the endgame that follows it (learned from B in other positions). Two informed positions predict the opposite: the composition of two facts fails in transformers \[11, 12\] and \[2\] obtains weak results; but chess transformers generalize rules to out-of-distribution positions \[15\]. The mixture theory predicts nothing here because the training signal is *silent*, not contradictory, in the positions in question.

### More formal definition

* Each game is played by a single family on both sides.
* **Family A** (fraction `f_A` of the games): optimal move at random at every `t < N`. The sequence is **truncated** at move `N`: it does not include the rest of the game or the result token (it is filled with `PAD`).
* **Family B** (fraction `1 − f_A`): at `t < N` it plays a random legal column within the set allowed by its **style**; at `t ≥ N` it plays an optimal move at random until the end, with result token.
  Styles of B, ordered by severity of the shift with respect to the optimal openings:
  * `optimal`: optimal move at random (control: the endgames after optimal openings *are* in the support).
  * `random`: uniform legal column.
  * `no center`: columns `{1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7}`.
  * `edges`: columns `{1, 2, 6, 7}`. It is the main style.
  If no allowed column is legal, it plays any legal one.
* Constants: `N = 8`, `f_A = 0.5`. TODO: `N = 8` cuts the opening early; the exploration tried `N = 14` with no change in the penalty. Decide whether to report it as robustness.
* There is no random error in either family: the experiment isolates the support gap, without error cancellation on top.

### Configurations

| Constants | Variables |
|---|---|
| `N = 8`, `f_A = 0.5` (except in the A-only and B-only controls), data and regime of exp. 1 | Four main configurations: **control** (A + B `optimal`), **A + B `edges`**, **A only** (`f_A = 1`), **B only `edges`** (`f_A = 0`) |
| | Optional, severity gradient: A + B `random`, A + B `no center` (2 more configurations) |

### What is "expert" and what is "to transcend" here

Unlike experiments 1 to 3, there is no expert defined over the whole game with which to compare move by move: A does not know how to play endgames and B does not know how to open. That is why two objects are distinguished:

* **Transfer** (move by move, in the endgame): `acc` of the model in states with `t ≥ N` compared with that of the control. The reference expert in the endgame is B, which there is optimal (`acc = 1`); what is measured is not transcendence over B but how much is lost because of the support gap, `Δ = acc(configuration) − acc(control)`.
* **Composition** (full game): the model transcends if, on its own trajectories, it plays the entire game better than either of the two families. It is the definition of \[1\] with `p_test` = state distribution of the model itself. Operationally: `score` against the perfect player greater than that of A-only and B-only, and high `acc` in all phases of its own games.

### Evaluation (what changes with respect to the common metrics)

`acc_τ` and `E[r]_τ` in the endgame (`t ≥ N`) are computed over three state distributions, at `τ → 0` and `τ = 1`:

1. **Endgames in support**: states with `t ≥ N` from test games generated by the configuration's own family B (different seed). It verifies that the model learned the endgame where it saw it.
2. **Endgames after optimal openings** (out of support): states with `t ≥ N` from test games generated by the control family (optimal opening + optimal endgame). None of these positions appears in the training of the configurations with B `edges`, `no center` or `random`. It is the main transfer metric.
3. **Own play**: the states that the model reaches in 300 games **against the perfect player** (solver), 150 with each color. `acc` by phase over those trajectories and `score`.
   **Note:** here the opponent is the perfect player and not the population bot, because there is no population policy defined over the whole game. Since the perfect first player always wins at Connect4, the maximum achievable `score` is `0.5` (winning all as first player, losing all as second). A `score` of `0.35` is equivalent to winning 70 % of the games as first player. The result with each color is also reported separately.

In addition, for the seen/unseen label of experiment 3, the fraction of states of distribution 2 that appear in the training of each configuration is reported. It should be ≈ 0 in the configurations with restricted style; it is the check that the support gap is real.

### Predictions (recorded before running)

* **Transfer with a small, graded penalty**: `acc` on distribution 2 drops relative to the control in the order `random < no center < edges`, and the penalty with `edges` is less than `0.03`. There is no collapse in any configuration.
* **B only transfers the same as A + B** on distribution 2: the data from A contribute nothing to the endgame. What A contributes is the opening.
* **B only loses all games** against the perfect player (it opens along the edges). **A only collapses in the endgame** (`acc` falls with `t`, beyond where it saw data). **A + B plays the full game**: opening and endgame close to 1, `score` close to `0.35`. Neither of the two families can do that: it is composition.
* The penalty does not depend on the model size, the amount of data or `N`. TODO: decide whether any of these robustness checks is run (the exploration ran them: models of 0.1M and 0.8M, 20K games, `N = 14`).
* Expected side observation: A only, without having seen a move beyond `N`, plays the middlegame reasonably before collapsing in the endgame: it extrapolates the structure of the optimal opening beyond its support.

### Limitation to put in writing

Connect4 is a fully observable game where the relevant board features are local; in that domain the optimistic prediction (transfer) was the plausible one. The result says that the learned endgame policy is a function of the board and not a table over B's trajectories, even though the model only sees sequences and has to build that representation from edge histories. It says nothing about composing declarative knowledge, where the results of \[2\] remain the relevant evidence.

# Total grid and execution order

| Experiment | New runs | Reuses |
|---|---|---|
| 2: selection | 7 (+1 optional control) | nothing |
| 3: seen / unseen | 2 (`N = 20K` and `320K` with `P = 0`) | the models of exp. 1 (`P = 0`, `P = 1` rule, `P = 1` hash) |
| 4: composition | 4 (+2 severity gradient) | nothing |

Suggested order, following the risk-first principle: 3 first (it trains almost nothing and its result conditions how experiment 1 is read), then 2, then 4. Experiment 4 is the longest and the most independent: its expert pipeline (families, truncation) and its evaluation (three distributions, perfect opponent) are the only ones that share nothing with experiment 1 beyond the common elements.
